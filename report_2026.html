Spring 2026 in Washington: about normal, leaning early

Across 24 indicator species, flowering began about 3.5 days earlier than the 2017–2025 average. That is inside the ±7-day range we call “normal.” Mid- and high-elevation sites west of the Cascades were the exception: they bloomed clearly early.

How the call changed over the season

The live dashboard said early from launch on Feb 28 until Jun 30, peaking at -35 days in March. As more of the season came in, the number kept drifting back toward zero.

Hover the line for daily values. The line is the number the dashboard actually published each day, which includes the data bug described below. The blue dot is the final value after the fix.

Why early readings overstated earliness. Each species’ onset is the 20th-percentile date of that year’s flowering observations. In March, only the first blooms have been seen, so the 20th percentile of a partial season sits much earlier than it will once the full season is in. The baseline uses complete seasons, so early-season comparisons are biased toward “early.” In-season numbers are best read as provisional until roughly June.

Final result by species

early (≤ −7 d) normal late (≥ +7 d) hollow = fewer than 20 observations in 2026

Most species landed near their usual timing. The strongest early signals came from foothill desert-parsley (−46 d, but only 12 observations), Oregon grape (−24 d) and red-flowering currant (−13 d, 210 observations). All four “late” species rest on 16 or fewer observations, and red alder and bitter cherry on just 2–3, so treat those calls as weak.

Corrections made for this final report

  • Missing 2026 data for three very common species. The script fetched observations oldest-first and stopped at 1,400 records. Pacific trillium (4,568 records since 2017), Pacific bleeding heart and red-flowering currant all exceed that, so their 2026 observations were cut off. Trillium and bleeding heart showed zero 2026 blooms all season. Red-flowering currant lost its 2026 data on Nov 17, when newly annotated older records pushed it past the cap; that caused the jump in the chart above. Because the “no blooms yet” rule counts each missing day as one more day late, these species reached +180 to +220 days. The cap is now 9,800 (the iNaturalist API limit), and all three have real data: trillium +0.1 d, bleeding heart −5.7 d, currant −13.3 d.
  • Elevation fill skipped when the lookup budget ran out. When a run used up its daily allowance of new elevation lookups, it also stopped applying elevations already in the cache, so later species fell into “unknown elevation” zones. Cached elevations are now always applied.

Net effect: the published final number moved from -2.4 to -3.5 days. The overall call (normal) did not change.

About this project

Bloom onset comes from research-grade iNaturalist observations in Washington annotated as flowering. Each species and region is compared with its own 2017–2025 median. Regions are east/west of the Cascades by elevation band. Results reflect where and when people photograph flowers as well as when plants bloom. Full method: README · long-term herbarium context · daily season history (CSV).
